# Module 1: The job

**Purpose:** Know exactly what the skill is for before writing a word of it. A skill
that triggers on the wrong request, or does a vaguer job than the person meant, is
worse than none: it is confidently wrong every time.

## Start from what just happened

Most skills are born at the end of a piece of work — "that was good, do it like that
every time". When that is the case, the conversation is your first draft: the steps
you took, the questions you had to ask, the corrections the person made. Name them
back and ask what to keep.

## Ask, one question at a time

Stop as soon as you can answer all five in the person's own words:

1. **What is the job?** One sentence a colleague would understand.
2. **When should it start?** The phrases the person actually uses when they want it.
   Ask for three. These become the trigger phrases in the description.
3. **What does it need first?** Inputs, files, figures, who it is for.
4. **What does done look like?** The deliverable, its shape, how long, in what voice.
5. **What must it never do?** The mistake they corrected, the line it must not cross.

Ask nothing the Business Brain already answers.

## Three checks before you go on

- **Is this a whole role?** Several unrelated jobs, a new kind of work nobody on the
  roster does, a need for tools nobody has — that is an employee, not a skill. Hand to
  `design-your-ai-employee` and say why.
- **Does a skill already do it?** If one of the skills you hold (or the AI COO's roster
  names) covers it, say which and stop. A near-duplicate skill splits the work and
  makes both worse.
- **Is it one job?** If the answers describe two jobs, write two skills, one at a time.
