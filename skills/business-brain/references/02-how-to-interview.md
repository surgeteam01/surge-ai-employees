# Module 2: How to interview

**Purpose:** The conduct that makes the answers worth having. The questions are in Module 1; this is how to ask them so the file ends up saying what the founder actually thinks, in their own words, and nothing they did not say.

## The rules

1. **Start from what exists.** If `BUSINESS-BRAIN.md` is present (Module 3 says where to look), read it first and say what it already answers. Ask only what is `_unanswered_` or what the founder says has changed. Restarting an interview a file already holds is the failure this skill exists to remove.
2. **One question at a time, and wait.** A numbered list of forty questions is a form, not an interview, and it gets abandoned. Ask, wait for the answer, then ask the next.
3. **Parts in order, and say which part you are on.** "Next is *Brand voice and style* — how the business sounds." The founder should always know where they are and roughly how far there is to go.
4. **Required first within a part.** Later skills cannot do without them. Optional questions can be offered as "want to do these now, or come back later?"
5. **Accept "skip" and "I don't know".** Record nothing for that field — it stays `_unanswered_`. Never fill it in yourself. An invented answer is indistinguishable afterwards from one the founder gave, and every later skill will build on it.
6. **When an answer is vague, ask one follow-up for the specific.** "Small businesses" → *which* small businesses — an industry, a size, a role? "Reasonably priced" → an actual number. "Friendly" → the real words they use with a client. A brain full of generalities produces generic work later.
7. **Slow down on the boundary questions.** What they do *not* cover, their deal-breakers, what to push back on, what not to let them do. People rush these and they are the answers that stop later work agreeing to things this business does not want. Give them room; ask "anything else?" once.
8. **Offer choices when you can name plausible answers — but only from a source.** Something they said earlier, something their own website says, or a genuinely standard option in their industry. Offer them as a pick-list and let the founder tick what is true and add what is missing. Never fill a list with plausible-sounding guesses, and never mark one "recommended" to nudge a tidy answer.
9. **A public website may be read, never trusted.** If the founder gives one, use what it actually says to *propose* answers, say which details came from the site, and ask them to confirm before writing. An inference from colours, imagery or copy is not their answer until they say it is.
10. **Read the whole thing back before writing.** In your own words, part by part, briefly. Fix what they correct. Only then write the file.
11. **Record both halves of a picked answer.** When a founder picks from a list and adds their own words, the free-text half is usually the specific one. Keep both.

## Pacing

Aim for the required questions in the first sitting — roughly fifteen minutes — and say so at the start: "About fifteen minutes for the essentials; the rest whenever you like." A founder who knows the size of the task finishes it.

## Output format

The file in Module 1's shape, written where Module 3 says. Then, in the conversation, a two-line summary: which parts are complete, which questions are still `_unanswered_`, and the one-line instruction for next time ("open with *what do you know about my business?* and I will read it back").

## Handoff

Module 3 places the file. Module 4 is for later — when the business has moved on, or when another skill hands back something the brain should keep.
